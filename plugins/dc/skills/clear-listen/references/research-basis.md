# Research basis for the clear-listen rules

This file holds the evidence behind the clear-listen method. SKILL.md gives the rules. This file explains why each rule exists, how strong its support is, and where the evidence stops.

The listener clear-listen serves is intelligent and works in IT, but loses focus easily (ADHD tendencies), is often tired or in a noisy place, and often listens while doing something else. The audio is in Brazilian Portuguese, his native language.

Version 2, 8 October 2026. Version 1 came from about 20 searches and abstracts only. Version 2 adds a deep research pass that checked the first sources, corrected several of them, and widened the map. The full report is the document "Clear-listen: Evidence Base for Explanatory Audio for an Adult Listener with ADHD Tendencies". Most sources were still read as abstracts or publisher pages, so check an exact effect size before quoting it elsewhere.

**The limit that shapes everything:** no controlled study tests podcast, audiobook or narration comprehension in adults with ADHD. The rules are assembled from four kinds of evidence, marked in each section:
- **Direct**: adults with ADHD.
- **General**: adults or students without ADHD.
- **Analogy**: another population, task or medium.
- **Practice**: expert guidance with no controlled trial.

## Quick map: which evidence backs which rule

- Role of audio (gist and review, not reasoning): reading versus listening meta-analysis; transient information effect.
- Rule 1 (short parts, spoken map, core idea first, re-entry line): segmenting meta-analysis; early mind wandering; ADHD mind-wandering mediation study.
- Rule 2 (one idea per sentence, no parentheses): transient information effect; ADHD working memory; broadcast practice.
- Rule 3 (say the structure): signaling principle; discourse signaling cues.
- Rule 4 (repeat on purpose, in different words): transient information effect; COGA "do not rely on memory".
- Rule 5 (say every "because"): ADHD story-comprehension studies; the reading advantage on inferential questions.
- Rule 6 (retrieve, in the head): interpolated testing; testing effect in students with ADHD; covert retrieval.
- Rule 7 (few numbers, in words): working-memory load; broadcast practice.
- Rule 8 (talk to one person): personalization principle.
- Rule 9 (moderate pace, pauses, speed control): speech-rate studies; Brazilian Portuguese rate data; playback-speed meta-analysis.
- Rule 10 (moderate expression, varied): pitch-range studies; the expressive balance effect.
- Rule 11 (voice only, clean signal): coherence principle; speech in noise in ADHD; WCAG 1.4.7.
- Rule 12 (one constant voice per role, measure the rate): text-to-speech and learning studies; voice-quality studies.
- Rule 13 (a second voice must earn its place): vicarious learning; deep questions; misconceptions; orienting to voice changes.
- Rule 14 (novelty from form, rotated): seductive details; orienting and habituation.
- Rule 15 (land the ending, short review): early mind wandering; re-watching study.

## 1. Reading versus listening

**General.** A meta-analysis of 46 studies (4,687 participants) found no reliable overall difference between reading and listening comprehension (g = 0.07, not significant). Reading was better when the reader set the pace, and for inferential comprehension. Later reviews describe reading as better for expository text, not for narrative. Reading while listening gave only a trivial benefit over reading alone.

Meaning: explanatory audio sits in the unfavorable cell. It is expository, the audio sets the pace, and understanding needs inference. So the script must supply what the page gives for free, and state conclusions instead of leaving them to inference.

**Not known:** listening versus reading in adults with ADHD. No study was found.

## 2. Speech is transient, and segmenting is the best-supported fix

**General.** In the transient information effect, a long spoken explanation loses to the same explanation in writing, and segmentation reduces the loss (carried over from version 1, not re-checked).

**General, strong.** A meta-analysis of the segmenting effect (56 investigations, 88 comparisons) found small to medium gains for retention and transfer, and lower cognitive load. The gains held when the system set the segments, so the listener does not need a pause button. Learners with high prior knowledge gained more on retention, which fits an expert listener. A review of reviews also found solid support for coherence (cutting seductive details) and personalization (conversational style).

Meaning: short parts, with a real pause between them, are the core of the method. (Rules 1, 2, 4, 8, 14.)

Limit: most segmenting studies used narrated animations with very short segments. No study gives the best part length for audio alone. Video engagement drops after about six minutes, and one lecture study used segments of about five minutes. So 3 to 4 minutes is a default to test, not a finding.

## 3. Mind wandering

**General.** In a within-subject study, listening produced the most mind wandering and the poorest memory, compared with reading silently and reading aloud.

**Direct (reading, not listening).** In 210 adults, about half with an ADHD diagnosis, the ADHD group understood a long expository text less well. Mind wandering during the task explained that gap. Step-by-step scaffolding made comprehension equal across groups. This is the closest direct evidence for the whole approach: the problem is wandering, and structure fixes it.

**Direct.** Adults with ADHD mind-wander more than controls even when task performance is matched, and the wandering is mostly spontaneous.

**General, with ADHD-type symptoms.** Mind wandering in the first half of a task was linked to poor comprehension more strongly than mind wandering overall.

Meaning: put the core idea in the first 30 seconds, and re-anchor it at the start of every part. (Rule 1.) This rule is new in version 2.

## 4. Retrieval and questions

**General.** Short tests between segments of a 21-minute lecture cut mind wandering from about 40 percent of checks to about 19 percent, raised note-taking, and improved the final test. Correction to version 1: a later study by the same group did not find the mind-wandering difference, although testing still improved how well learners connected the ideas. So the learning benefit is solid, and the attention benefit is probable.

**Direct.** In college students with ADHD, retrieval practice helped about as much as in their peers in three studies. Two studies found no benefit. Students with ADHD also test themselves less on their own.

**General.** Prequestions before a video segment improve learning (carried over). Answering silently in the head has been reported to work about as well as answering aloud for simple material (carried over, weak).

Meaning: one question per part, answerable in the head, with a pause. (Rule 6.) No study has tested whether such prompts reduce mind wandering in ADHD.

## 5. Other ADHD evidence relevant to listening

- **Direct.** Young adults with ADHD had more trouble with speech in noise, linked to working memory. This is about hearing sentences, not about understanding long explanations.
- **Analogy (children).** Children with ADHD showed weaker listening comprehension linked to working memory, and kept fewer causal links and goals from stories (carried over).
- **Analogy (reaction-time tasks).** Slow event rates worsen ADHD performance, and extra stimulation helped on an auditory vigilance task (carried over). Applied to speech pace, this is a hypothesis.
- **Direct, weak.** White or pink noise gave a small gain on attention tasks in youth with ADHD symptoms (g about 0.25, 13 studies) and a small loss without ADHD. The tasks were not listening comprehension, and newer work questions the proposed mechanism. One study found that a speech-recognition gap in children with attention problems disappeared under 65 decibels of noise.

Meaning: protect working memory, say causes and goals, keep the signal clean, avoid slow flat delivery. Noise is the listener's own experiment, never part of the audio. (Rules 2, 5, 9, 10, 11.)

## 6. Pace, and why Portuguese needs its own number

**General.** In Spanish radio news, recognition was best between 170 words per minute (dense items) and 190 (light items). Below 170, attention dropped. Most radio manuals recommend 160 to 180. Studies of ads also found memory best at moderate speed.

**Portuguese data.** In matched texts read aloud, Portuguese had about 2.1 syllables per word, English about 1.4, and Spanish about 2.4. Trainee radio announcers in Brazil read news at 164 words per minute, which is about 6 syllables per second. Spontaneous Brazilian speech is much slower, about 90 words per minute with pauses.

**Conversion.** Converted by content, the Spanish optimum of 170 to 190 becomes about 159 to 178 words per minute in Brazilian Portuguese. English norms of 160 to 180 become about 139 to 157. The measured Brazilian news rate, 164, sits between.

Correction to version 1: it proposed 165 to 180 and called 150 "probably too slow". The better default is **160 words per minute, range 150 to 175**, about 5.5 to 6 syllables per second with pauses. Confidence is medium to low, because these are conversions and not tested optima.

**Playback speed, general.** A meta-analysis of 24 studies found a small cost at 1.5 times speed and below, and a larger cost at 2 times. All of it used video with visuals that back up missed speech, so audio alone is probably less tolerant. Letting learners control speed goes with less mind wandering (correlational).

**Pauses.** No controlled study of pause length in explanatory audio was found. Pause values in SKILL.md are practice defaults.

**Not known:** the best rate for a listener with ADHD. Claims that "most people with ADHD prefer 1.5 times" come from vendor sites, not from studies.

## 7. Voice and expression

- **General.** Pitch-range variation improved attention and recall in radio messages (carried over). A 2022 study found the best credibility and attention with moderate intonation and medium rate, not with maximum expression.
- **General.** A modern text-to-speech voice gave learning equal to a human voice in two studies, though learners liked the human voice more. Limit: English voices, on video agents. Neural Portuguese voices are untested.
- **General, weak.** Studies of AI-generated two-host podcasts are mostly opinion surveys. Some students call the voices robotic.
- **Analogy (children).** A hoarse voice lowered listening comprehension (carried over).

Meaning: moderate, varied expression, and a clean voice checked by ear. (Rules 10, 12.)

## 8. Two voices

- **General.** In radio messages, a change of voice triggers an orienting response, and memory improves for what follows it. In two-minute dialogues the response did not wear off after six changes. A bigger pitch difference between the voices strengthens it, and early changes work best.
- **General.** The same sound effect repeated does wear off.
- **Analogy (video).** Learners gained more from an observed dialogue than from a monologue when the dialogue carried deep "why" and "how" questions before the explanation, or voiced a misconception that was then corrected. One study found no general advantage for dialogue. (Carried over.)

Meaning: two voices help through the voice change and through what the second voice says. Chat adds load. (Rules 13, 14.) Whether two hosts beat one narrator in audio alone is untested.

## 9. Background sound

**General, strong.** Adding music and sounds to a narrated lesson hurt recall and transfer (carried over), and the coherence principle holds across reviews. WCAG 2.2 criterion 1.4.7 asks for no background sound, or at least 20 decibels below speech.

Meaning: voice only. (Rule 11.)

## 10. Structure you can hear

Discourse signaling cues improved lecture recall, mostly in second-language listeners (carried over). For native listeners the support is by analogy to the signaling principle.

Meaning: speak the headings. (Rule 3.)

## 11. Standards and practice

- Public radio and public health audio guides agree on: one thought per sentence, a sentence fits one breath, everyday words, few numbers, speak to one person, read aloud before recording.
- W3C COGA Objective 6: do not rely on the user's memory.
- Brazil's Lei 15.263 of 2025 (plain language) is written for written text. Its techniques are direct order, short sentences, common words, explained jargon, and the most important information first. No clause about spoken communication was found. The principles carry over. The law is not audio guidance.

## 12. Listening while moving

In children with ADHD, more movement went with better working-memory performance (carried over). The cost of walking or commuting on comprehension of explanatory audio is not known. Practical consequence: write for interruptions, and ask questions that need no hands.

## Trade-offs to weigh, not ignore

- **Repetition versus an expert's patience.** Restate at part boundaries, in new words. Never echo.
- **Same name, different sentence.** One name per thing, always. Vary the sentence that restates the key point.
- **Calm versus flat.** Moderate expression beats both extremes.
- **Pace versus density.** Hold the moderate rate, move the densest content to the written text, and add pauses.
- **Questions versus flow.** One question per part. More becomes a quiz.
- **Novelty versus coherence.** Novelty from form only, and rotated.
- **One narrator versus voice changes.** Version 1 said "one constant narrator". Voice changes help attention, so the rule is now one constant voice per role.
- **Dialogue versus length.** Dialogue is longer. Use it where a misconception exists.

## Certainty and caveats

- High certainty: segmenting helps; background sound under speech hurts; reading beats listening for self-paced, expository, inferential content; retrieval practice improves learning.
- Medium certainty: mind wandering is the mechanism of the ADHD comprehension gap (shown for reading); interpolated questions reduce mind wandering; moderate rate and moderate expression are best; a voice change resets attention; modern synthetic voices are acceptable.
- Low certainty or practice defaults: every number in SKILL.md (160 words per minute, parts of 3 to 4 minutes, pause lengths, 8 to 15 words, stressed words per sentence).
- Not known: listening versus reading in adult ADHD; best rate for explanatory audio in Brazilian Portuguese; speed tolerance for audio alone; two hosts versus narration in audio; whether prompts reduce mind wandering in ADHD; audio first or text first; fatigue from a synthetic voice over 20 minutes.

## Tests to run with the real listener

See `tests.md`, tests 4 to 10. Use matched topics, random order, free recall after 24 hours, and at least 4 sessions per condition.
